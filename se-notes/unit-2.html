Unit 2: Requirement Engineering

How requirements are gathered, analysed, modelled and written down in the SRS.

1Requirements Engineering

Requirements Engineering (RE) is the process of identifying, eliciting, analysing, specifying, validating and managing the needs and expectations of stakeholders for a software system.
  • It establishes the services the customer needs from the system and the constraints under which it operates and is developed.
  • The requirements themselves are the descriptions of those services and constraints, produced during the RE process.
  • It is the bridge between what the customer wants and what the developers build.

2What is a Requirement?

A requirement is a description of a service a software system must provide, or a constraint under which it must operate. In IEEE terms, it is a condition or capability a user needs to solve a problem or achieve an objective.
  • Requirements range from high-level abstract statements to detailed mathematical functional specifications.

3Types: User vs System Requirements

User RequirementsSystem Requirements

4Functional & Non-functional Requirements

Functional requirements

Functional requirements describe the services or functions the system must provide: how it reacts to inputs and how it behaves in particular situations. They are the "whats" of the system.
  • They depend on the type of software, the expected users and the type of system.
  • User-level ones are high-level. System-level ones describe services in detail.
  • They may also say what the system should not do.
  • Examples: authenticate the user at login · shut the system down during a cyber attack · send a verification email when a user registers.

Non-functional requirements

Non-functional requirements are the quality constraints the system must satisfy, such as reliability, response time, maintainability, scalability, portability and storage. They usually apply to the system as a whole.
  • They include constraints like I/O device capability, timing, standards and the development process (e.g. a required IDE or language).
  • They can be more critical than functional ones. If they aren't met, the system may be useless.
  • Examples: each request must be processed within 10 seconds · the site must load in 3 seconds with more than 10,000 simultaneous users.

Comparison

FunctionalNon-functional
What the system doesHow well it does it
Specific features or servicesQuality attributes and constraints
Defined at component levelApplied to the whole system
E.g. "Load the page when the button is clicked"E.g. "The page must load within 2 seconds"

Example: "Clicking a button loads a page" is functional. "How fast the page loads" is non-functional. A slow page gives a poor experience even though the functional requirement is met.

5Requirements Engineering Process: Main Activities

Elicitation → Specification → Verification & Validation → Management ▲ │ └──────────────┘ (specification may trigger more elicitation)

1. Requirements elicitation

  • Gathering knowledge about the project domain and its requirements.
  • Sources: customers, business manuals, existing software of the same type, standards, other stakeholders.
  • Techniques: interviews, brainstorming, task analysis, the Delphi technique, prototyping.
  • It doesn't produce formal models. It widens the analyst's knowledge as input for the next stage.

2. Requirements specification

  • Produces formal requirement models covering all functional and non-functional requirements and constraints.
  • Models used: ER diagrams, data flow diagrams (DFDs), function decomposition diagrams (FDDs), data dictionaries.

3. Requirements verification & validation

  • Verification: the software correctly implements a specific function ("Are we building the product right?").
  • Validation: the software built is traceable to customer requirements ("Are we building the right product?").
  • If requirements aren't validated, errors spread to later stages and cause rework.
  • Checks: requirements are consistent (no conflicts), complete and achievable. Methods: reviews, buddy checks, test cases.

4. Requirements management

  • Analysing, documenting, tracking, prioritising and agreeing on requirements, and controlling communication with stakeholders.
  • Handles the changing nature of requirements. The SRS should be as modifiable as possible.

6Requirements Engineering Tasks (Seven Tasks)

Inception → Elicitation → Elaboration → Negotiation → Specification → Validation → Requirements Management

Mnemonic: "I Eat Every Nice Sandwich Very Merrily": Inception, Elicitation, Elaboration, Negotiation, Specification, Validation, Management.

7Problems of Requirements Analysis

  • Stakeholders don't know what they really want.
  • Stakeholders express requirements in their own terms.
  • Different stakeholders may have conflicting requirements.
  • Organisational and political factors may influence requirements.
  • Requirements change during analysis. New stakeholders may appear, and the business environment may change.

8Requirement Analysis Process

The analysis process is a cycle of activities, repeated until the requirements are complete:

Domain Understanding → Requirements Collection → Classification ▲ │ Requirements Checking ← Prioritisation ← Conflict Resolution

Domain understandingLearn the application domain.
Requirements collectionGather requirements from stakeholders.
ClassificationOrganise the requirements into related groups.
Conflict resolutionResolve conflicting requirements.
PrioritisationIdentify the most important requirements.
Requirements checkingCheck that requirements are complete, consistent and match what stakeholders want.

9Negotiating Requirements

Negotiation is the collaborative process of resolving conflicts and aligning stakeholder expectations to reach agreement on requirements, scope, budget, timeline and resources.
  • It involves discussion, compromise and prioritisation.
  • Stakeholders balance functionality and performance against cost and time-to-market.
  • The goal is a "win-win" outcome: stakeholders get a system that meets most of their needs, and developers work within realistic budgets and schedules.

Boehm's negotiation activities (at the start of each iteration)

  1. Identify the key stakeholders.
  2. Determine each stakeholder's "win conditions".
  3. Negotiate those win conditions into a set of win-win conditions for everyone.

10Validating the Requirements Model

As the model is built, each part is checked for consistency, omissions and ambiguity. Stakeholders prioritise the requirements and bundle them into packages for each software increment.

Questions asked during validation

  • Is each requirement consistent with the overall system objectives?
  • Is each requirement at the right level of abstraction?
  • Is the requirement really necessary, or just an optional add-on?
  • Is each requirement bounded and unambiguous?
  • Is each requirement attributed, with a source noted?
  • Do any requirements conflict with others?
  • Is each requirement achievable in the technical environment?
  • Is each requirement testable once implemented?

11Analysis Modelling

An analysis model is a technical representation of the system. It links the system description to the design model. It defines the system's information, behaviour and functions, which are then translated into architecture, interface and component design.
System Description ──▶ ANALYSIS MODEL ──▶ Design Model

Objectives

  • Describe what the customer requires.
  • Establish a basis for creating the software design.
  • Define a set of requirements that can be validated once the software is built.

12Elements of the Analysis Model (Structured Analysis)

The classic structure has the Data Dictionary at the core, surrounded by three diagrams, each with its own specification:

Data Object Description │ ERD (data model) │ PSPEC ── DFD ──── DATA DICTIONARY ──── STD ── CSPEC (functional model) (core) (behavioural model)
ElementDescription
Data DictionaryA central repository describing every data object used or produced by the software. It is the most crucial element.
Entity Relationship Diagram (ERD)Shows the relationships between data objects. It is used for data modelling and is the basis for data design.
Data Object DescriptionGives complete details of each data object in the ERD and all its attributes.
Data Flow Diagram (DFD)Shows the functions that transform data as it moves from input to output. It is the basis for function modelling.
Process Specification (PSPEC)Describes each function in the DFD: its input, the algorithm applied, its output, and any performance limits or constraints.
State Transition Diagram (STD)Shows the system's states and the events that cause it to change state, plus the actions taken. It models behaviour.
Control Specification (CSPEC)Extra information on control: how the software behaves when an event occurs, and which processes are invoked.

13Building the Analysis Model

Building the analysis model means translating high-level user requirements into a technical representation, using diagrams and text to show the system's data, functions and behaviour.

Steps

  1. Understand the requirements gathered from the customer.
  2. Choose an approach, such as class-based or flow-oriented.
  3. Identify the key models needed for data, functions and behaviour.
  4. Create the model with use cases, class diagrams, DFDs, state diagrams and so on.
  5. Iterate and refine as more information comes in.

Analysis rules of thumb

  • Focus on requirements visible in the business domain, at a high level of abstraction. Skip the details.
  • Every element should add to the understanding of the information, function and behaviour.
  • Delay infrastructure and non-functional models until design. For example, a database is needed, but its classes and functions aren't modelled yet.
  • Keep coupling (interconnections between modules) to a minimum.
  • The model should give value to all stakeholders.
  • Keep the model as simple as possible.

14Data Modeling

Data modeling is where analysis modelling starts. The engineer defines all the data objects in the system, their attributes and the relationships between them, usually as an ERD.

1. Data objects

  • A representation of composite information: something with several properties or attributes.
  • It can be an external entity, a thing, an occurrence, a role, an organisational unit, a place or a structure.
  • Example: height alone is a single value, so it is not a data object. Dimensions (height, width and depth) is a data object.
  • A data object holds data only. It has no reference to operations, which is unlike a class in OOP.

2. Attributes

Attributes define the properties of a data object. They are used to:

  • Name an instance of the object.
  • Describe the instance.
  • Reference another instance in another table.

One or more attributes must be the identifier (the key). Example: a Car has ID#, make, model, body type and colour.

3. Relationships

  • Show how data objects are connected. Example: a bookstore orders, displays, stocks, sells and returns books.
  • Object/relationship pairs are bidirectional, so they can be read in either direction.

15Cardinality & Modality

Cardinality

Cardinality is the number of occurrences of one object that can be related to the number of occurrences of another. It's expressed as "one" or "many".
TypeMeaningExample
One-to-one (1:1)One instance relates to one instanceOne employee has one ID
One-to-many (1:N)One instance relates to manyOne college has many departments
Many-to-many (M:N)Many relate to manyMany customers order many products

Modality

Modality says whether a relationship is optional or mandatory.
  • Modality = 0: the relationship is optional, shown by a circle (O) in the ERD.
  • Modality = 1: the relationship is mandatory, shown by a bar (|) in the ERD.

Example: telephone company field service

CUSTOMER ──||──────── is provided with ────────O<── REPAIR ACTION one, mandatory zero or many (optional)
  • Cardinality (1:N): one customer can get zero or many repair actions (the "crow's foot" means many).
  • Modality: the bar on the customer side means a customer must exist for a repair action. The circle on the repair side means there may be no repair action (a simple problem may not need one).

16Analysis Modeling Approaches (Elements)

There are two broad schools: Structured Analysis (data and process kept separate) and Object-Oriented Analysis (data and process combined in objects). The analysis model has four kinds of elements:

ElementWhat it showsDiagrams
Scenario-basedThe system from the user's point of viewUse case text and diagrams, user stories, activity diagrams, swim-lane diagrams
Class-basedThe objects the system manipulates: attributes, relationships, collaborationClass diagrams, analysis packages, CRC models, collaboration diagrams
BehaviouralThe system's states and how external events change themState diagrams, sequence diagrams
Flow-orientedHow data objects are transformed as they flow through functionsData flow diagrams, control flow diagrams, data structure diagrams, processing narratives
Remember: scenario = user's view · class = objects · behavioural = states and events · flow = data transformation.

17Object-Oriented Analysis (OOA)

Object-Oriented Analysis investigates the problem and its requirements (not the solution), with an emphasis on finding and describing the objects or concepts in the problem domain.

Purpose

  • How to characterise the system.
  • Which relevant objects exist and how they relate to each other.
  • How to model the problem so that an effective design can be created.
  • Examine the requirements and analyse their implications.

Intent

Define all the classes relevant to the problem, with their operations, attributes, relationships and behaviour. The tasks are:

  1. Know the basic user requirements.
  2. Identify the classes, with their attributes and methods.
  3. Specify the class hierarchy.
  4. Represent object-to-object relationships.
  5. Model object behaviour.
  6. Repeat tasks 1–5 until the model is complete.

Conventional (structured) vs OO analysis

Structured AnalysisObject-Oriented Analysis
Treats processes and data separatelyCombines data and process into objects
Uses DFD, ERD, STD, data dictionaryUses use cases, class diagrams, CRC, sequence diagrams
Top-down functional decompositionIdentifies objects and classes
Harder to reuseEncourages reuse (inheritance)

18Scenario-Based Modeling

Scenario-based modeling represents the system from the user's point of view, mainly with use cases. To start, list the functions or activities a specific actor performs.
  • Actor: anyone or anything outside the system that interacts with it (a user, another system or a device).
  • Use case: a sequence of actions that gives the actor a useful result.
  • Sources: the list of required functions, and conversations with stakeholders.

Example: airline ticket booking

┌──────── Booking System ────────┐ Passenger ───┼── Search flights │ │ ├── Book ticket ──(include)── Make payment │ ├── Cancel ticket │ └────────┼── Check flight status │ Admin ───────┼── Manage flights / schedules │ └─────────────────────────────────┘

19Flow-Oriented Modeling

Flow-oriented modeling shows how data objects are transformed as they move through the system: it accepts input, applies hardware, software or human elements to transform it, and produces output.

Elements

ElementDescription
Data Flow Diagram (DFD)A graphical technique for showing information flow. Data objects are labelled arrows, and transformations are circles (bubbles). It's drawn as a hierarchy of levels: Level 0 is the context diagram, then Level 1, Level 2 and so on.
Control Flow ModelNeeded for large, event-driven or real-time applications that produce control information rather than reports. An event is a boolean value (true/false, on/off, 1/0).
Control Specification (CSPEC)Represents system behaviour using a state diagram (states, transitions, events, activities).
Process Specification (PSPEC)Describes every process in the flow model, using narrative text, PDL (Program Design Language), equations, tables or UML activity diagrams.

20Class-Based Modeling

Class-based modeling represents the objects the system manipulates, the operations applied to them, and the relationships and collaborations between them.

Elements

Classes and objects · attributes · operations · CRC models (Class-Responsibility-Collaborator) · collaboration diagrams · packages.

Identifying classes

Do a grammatical parse: underline each noun or noun phrase in the problem statement and list them in a table. The nouns are the candidate classes.

Classes appear in these forms

FormExamples
External entitiesOther systems, people or devices that produce information
ThingsReports, displays, letters, signals
Occurrences or eventsA property transfer, completing a series of robot movements
RolesManager, engineer, salesperson
Organisational unitsDivision, group, team
PlacesManufacturing floor, loading dock
StructuresSensors, computers, vehicles

21Software Requirement Specification (SRS)

The SRS is an official document, written by the analyst before development starts, that describes the expected system's requirements in a set format. It turns the customer's everyday-language requirements into technical language the development team can use.

Description

  • Tells both the developer and the customer what will be implemented.
  • Especially useful when the software is built by an outside contractor.
  • Must include interfaces, functional capabilities, quality, reliability, privacy and so on.
  • Models used: ER diagrams, DFDs, function decomposition diagrams, data dictionaries.

Characteristics of a good SRS

CharacteristicMeaning
CompleteCovers all requirements.
